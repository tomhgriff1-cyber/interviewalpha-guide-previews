10 things that actually matter

Counts show how many of the 20 videos mention each idea. Agreement is useful, but it is not proof. Each card therefore has a separate evidence verdict.

01

Tailor the CV to each job, using honest language from the advert

Every video says to align content with the specific job. The useful version is selective editing, not rewriting everything or inventing experience.

20/20 videos
Supported

Across all 20. The National Careers Service also recommends tailoring to the job and highlighting requested skills.

02

Use a simple, single-column layout with real text

Keep important facts out of images, icons, skill bars and complex design elements. Simple does not mean careless. Clear hierarchy and white space still matter.

17/20 videos
Supported

Greenhouse specifically lists columns, tables, graphics, text boxes, headers and footers as potential parsing problems.

03

Show relevant achievements, not a list of duties

Choose the experience that proves fit for this job. Replace vague responsibilities with what changed because of your work.

16/20 videos
Supported

Strong creator agreement. This is about human review as well as matching. No format can compensate for weak evidence.

04

Give outcomes and numbers you can verify

Use scale, time saved, accuracy, speed or service outcomes where genuinely available. Do not manufacture a metric for every bullet.

13/20 videos
Supported

Teal usefully says the first bullet under each role matters most. Numbers are evidence, not an ATS spell.

05

Use familiar section names

Work experience, education and skills are safer than clever labels. Put the material the employer wants where a person and parser expect it.

11/20 videos
Supported

Greenhouse warns that unclear sections and inconsistent formats can cause partial parsing.

06

Keep dates, employers and job titles clear and consistent

Use a repeatable order, readable month and year dates, and recognisable job titles. Explain an unusual internal title honestly rather than inflating it.

9/20 videos
Supported

Workday says parsing varies with format and word order. Greenhouse says incomplete titles can cause incomplete results.

07

PDF and Word can both work. Follow the application instructions

Only 10 videos commit to file advice. Four favour PDF, two favour Word and four accept both or say to follow the advert. Never use an image-only file.

10/20 videos
Mixed

There is no universal winner. Greenhouse, Taleo and Lever accept text-based PDF and DOCX. Workday features differ, so check each form.

08

Check the text extraction and the autofilled application

Three videos suggest a plain-text or selectable-text test. One recruiter also says repeated manual correction is a warning that parsing is poor.

4/20 videos
Useful check

Lever recommends the selectable-text test. A clean copy is reassuring, but it cannot prove every employer system will parse the same way.

09

Keep it concise and weight recent, relevant experience

Recommendations vary: one page, two pages, and two to three pages. Relevance is the common thread. In the UK, one or two pages is a safer default.

8/20 videos
Mixed

Length is a human-reading convention, not an ATS rule. Academic, technical and creative applications may have different expectations.

10

Take application questions seriously

Basic eligibility answers can trigger configured rules. Complete the form carefully and honestly. A polished CV does not override contradictory answers.

4/20 videos
Supported

Greenhouse documents auto-reject based on custom question answers. Its standard application review also lets a recruiter advance or reject manually.

The most useful correction

A bad parse is not the same as a rejection

Most generic advice turns four separate things into one scary robot. Vendor documents show a more useful picture: a CV is parsed into fields, a recruiter can search or review candidates, optional matching can rank evidence, and employer rules can reject answers to application questions.

Greenhouse explicitly says a resume that fails to parse may simply be attached while its details are entered manually. The practical priority is clean, honest information, not learning secret tricks.

How screening actually works

Video evidence

20 videos discuss matching or keywords. Five explicitly warn that an ATS is not an autonomous decision-maker.

Supported An ATS is a database and workflow tool. Parsing, searching, ranking and automatic rejection are different operations.

The strongest mental model is four stages: parse the CV, find relevant profiles, review evidence, apply employer rules. Not every employer uses every stage. Greenhouse documents manual application review and optional question-based auto-reject. Workday documents skills matching and HiredScore comparison against requirements. That varies by product, subscription and configuration.

Formatting and layout

Video evidence

17 videos recommend simple layouts. 14 mention standard or readable fonts. Three suggest keeping a more creative version for direct human review.

Supported Use one column, real text, clear headings, a common font and consistent spacing. Mixed Columns and tables can corrupt extraction. They do not automatically trigger rejection in every system.

Keep the main CV deliberately plain. Put contact details in the document body, not a page header, footer or text box. Replace information carried only by graphics with words. A clearer layout also helps the recruiter scanning the result. Creative portfolios can remain separate links where relevant.

File type

Video evidence

10 videos discuss PDF or Word. Four recommend PDF, two recommend Word and four accept both or defer to the employer.

Mixed Both text-based PDF and DOCX are widely supported. The employer instruction and actual text layer matter more than dogma.

A PDF is not automatically unreadable. Greenhouse accepts PDF and DOCX for parsing, Oracle Taleo accepts both, and Lever accepts PDF and Microsoft Word. Lever specifically warns about image files. Workday has different parsers across features, including a documented feature that handles DOC, DOCX, HTM and TXT. The practical answer is: follow the form, use a real text document, and verify the extracted fields.

Keywords and tailoring

Video evidence

All 20 videos cover job-specific keywords. Six tell viewers to prioritise repeated terms. Seven warn against keyword stuffing. Two explicitly reject hidden white text.

Supported Use the employer language for skills and experience you genuinely have, in natural context. Myth Repeating a phrase many times is not a reliable universal ranking hack.

Start with essential requirements, then preferred criteria and responsibilities. Repeated terms can indicate priorities to a person, but they are not a guaranteed weighting formula. Use exact product or technical names where truthful, then back them with an example. Taleo supports exact and related-term searches. Lever searches parsed resume content. Modern matching can also compare skills more broadly, so literal copying alone is not the whole task.

“Keyword mapping” describes real work. “Keyword stuffing” adds phrases without evidence. Jeff Su, 3:58 onward Open at this moment

Content for the human reviewer

Video evidence

16 videos favour achievements over duties. 13 recommend verified numbers. Seven recommend ownership verbs. Five say to demonstrate soft skills through evidence.

Supported Specific, recent, relevant results make the CV easier to assess. Strong verbs help only when they accurately describe your contribution.

Use a simple structure: action, contribution and result. Lead each role with its strongest relevant bullet. Do not overload every line with numbers or adjectives. Two videos recommend briefly explaining gaps rather than leaving an unexplained story. One recruiter recommends trying a different CV version if results disappoint.

Sections, dates and contact details

Video evidence

11 videos recommend standard headings. Nine stress clear dates or employer details. Six specify reverse chronological work history. One warns against contact details in headers or footers.

Supported Conventional labels and consistent chronology reduce ambiguity for both parsers and readers. Mixed Whether contact details must be vertical or simply in the body is not consistent across creators.

Use a clean structure: name and contact details, a short role-relevant introduction if it adds value, work experience, education and skills. Put recent work first. Include month and year dates and use one pattern throughout. Keep your email professional. One video also recommends a professional file name, a small but sensible detail.

Checks before sending

Video evidence

Three videos demonstrate a plain-text or selectable-text test. One recommends checking repeated manual corrections. Two warn that third-party ATS scores are not employer scores.

Useful check Use extraction and autofilled fields to find obvious faults. Do not treat a third-party score as a pass mark.

Select and copy the CV text into a plain-text editor. Is the reading order intact? Are headings, dates and contact details present? Then check what the actual application autofills. This is a fault-finding check, not a compatibility certificate. Jobscan deserves credit for stating that its match score is its own analysis, not a secret score from the employer.

Length and local conventions

Video evidence

Eight videos recommend a page limit or recent-experience weighting. Three UK videos explicitly advise removing photos and personal details. Recommendations conflict on one versus two pages.

Mixed There is no universal page limit or global CV template. Local norms, experience and role matter.

UK guidance supports one or two pages for conventional CVs, with longer forms for some specialist applications. Leave out a photograph, date of birth, age, marital status and nationality unless a particular process genuinely requires otherwise. US sources often say resume and favour tighter one-page advice for early careers. Do not import every US convention into a UK guide.

Risky hacks to avoid

These either misrepresent the candidate, make the human review worse, or chase a technical loophole that vendor evidence does not support.

Do not trade credibility for a supposed shortcut

The safest optimisation is still accurate, readable evidence. These are the traps worth calling out plainly in the future guide.

01

White text or hidden job descriptions

The extracted text can expose the tactic. It is manipulation, not evidence of ability.

See the warning
02

Repeating keywords until the writing becomes unnatural

No reliable universal repetition rule exists. It makes the human review worse.

See the warning
03

Inflating a previous job title or adding skills you cannot evidence

Use a truthful clarification in brackets if needed. Never turn an assistant role into an executive one.

See the warning
04

Choosing a template for visual novelty rather than readable text

Skill bars, icons, text boxes and graphics can obscure or scramble important information.

See the warning
05

Optimising for a scanner number instead of the employer

A third-party score is only an estimate. Check the actual job and actual application fields.

See the warning
06

Leaving template placeholders or disguised fake data

Greenhouse says fake company names and similar placeholders can be skipped by its parser.

07

Being careless or dishonest on knockout questions

Employer-configured questions can trigger auto-reject. Keep CV and form answers consistent.

See the warning

Hiding a career gap behind vague dates

Brief context is better than inviting the recruiter to invent the story.

UK-specific notes

UK creators agree with much of the US technical advice, but conventions differ. A UK guide should not copy US length and personal-detail rules by default.

Same parsing basics. Different CV conventions.

Use “CV” in the guide while quoting US video titles as “resume”. Keep the technical checks, then layer UK expectations on top.

National Careers Service guidance
University of Bradford guidance

  • LengthOne or two pages is a sound conventional UK default. Academic and specialist CVs can be longer. The videos conflict between one, two and two to three pages.
  • PhotographsLeave a photo out for a conventional UK CV unless specifically required. It is not an ATS optimisation.
  • Personal detailsLeave out age, date of birth, marital status and nationality. Use a town or broad location rather than a full street address where suitable.
  • Right to workAnswer right-to-work and eligibility questions honestly. Do not add nationality to the CV as a substitute for the employer process.
  • Overseas qualificationsGive understandable UK context or equivalence where helpful. One UK video recommends this, and Bradford guidance supports it.
  • ReferencesDo not publish another person contact details without a good reason. References can usually wait until requested, though conventions vary for undergraduate placements.

How this becomes an InterviewAlpha guide

The editorial advantage is not another template. It is a calm explanation of what happens after upload, plus a way to learn which CV version actually earns replies.

Proposed guide title

CV screening software: how to get your CV read, parsed and noticed

Plain promise: understand the process, remove avoidable parsing problems, present relevant evidence and ignore the myths.

  1. What an ATS really doesParse, search, rank and employer rules. Explain that no two employers use exactly the same setup.
  2. The two checks every CV needsCan software extract it? Can a person see your fit quickly?
  3. A simple layout that travels wellOne-column annotated example, standard headings, readable font and consistent dates.
  4. PDF or Word?A small decision tree: employer instruction, real text layer, simple layout, and review of autofilled fields.
  5. Honest keyword mapping in ten minutesRequirements first, preferred criteria second, real examples to prove the match.
  6. Write for the human after the parserRelevant achievements, concise bullets and verified outcomes.
  7. The two-minute pre-submit checkCopy to plain text, inspect the application fields, check links, dates, spelling and eligibility answers.
  8. UK checklist and mythsLength, photos, personal details, white text, the 75% claim and third-party scores.

The missing step: measure what works

Only one of the 20 videos explicitly advises trying a different version when a CV is not producing results. That is a stronger editorial connection than promising a magical ATS score.

Instead of guessing which formatting rule made the difference, readers can try two CV versions with InterviewAlpha and see which gets more replies. Keep the advice on evidence and outcomes, not guaranteed interview claims.

Recommended visual assets: a four-stage screening diagram, a simple-versus-complex extraction example, a PDF or Word decision tree, and a one-page pre-submit checklist.

Method and limitations

Consensus coding: complete timestamped transcripts were read and advice manually grouped. Counts reflect a mention of the point, not endorsement of every stronger claim in that video. Similar advice with materially different meanings was kept separate. All counts are out of the same 20 videos.

Fact checks: vendor documentation establishes what named systems can do, not what every employer configures. Product features and documentation change. Lever pages were verified through indexed help-centre content because direct fetch returned 401 in this environment; all other primary sources were read directly. No statistics were invented. Unsupported video statistics were excluded or labelled.

Scope: this is editorial research for a future guide, not a promise of interview outcomes and not a substitute for a specific employer instruction. Creative, academic and highly specialist applications need their own guidance.
