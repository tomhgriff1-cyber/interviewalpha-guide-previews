# No interviews or no offers? Find where your job search is getting stuck

**Estimated reading time: 13 minutes**

You send applications, or you mean to, and nothing useful comes back. It is tempting to treat that as a verdict on the whole search, and then to change five things at once: a new CV or resume, a new title, a new board, a colder message to a stranger. When everything moves, you cannot tell what helped.

This guide separates the search into three stages and asks you to name the one that is actually quiet. There is no normal reply rate hiding underneath. [The National Careers Service, the government's careers advice service in England, notes that you might not hear back from every application, and that people often apply for several roles before an interview](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/application-forms). That is a description of how searching feels. It is not a quota, and it does not tell you whether your problem is the roles, the applications, or the interviews.

> **Keep this.** Silence is not a single problem. Find the stage that is quiet, then test one change against your own record.

**01**

## Three different problems

A job search that is "not working" is usually stuck in one of these places. They need different fixes. Rewriting your CV will not help if you are not finding roles you would accept. Interview practice will not help if nobody is inviting you to an interview.

1. **Finding suitable roles.** You are not seeing work you could do and would accept, or you are collecting adverts and not applying to them.

2. **Getting shortlisted.** You are applying to suitable roles, and those applications are not turning into a first conversation.

3. **Converting interviews.** You are getting interviews, and they are not turning into a further round or an offer.

A first conversation means a recruiter screen, a hiring manager call, an assessment, or any live step after the application. A saved job, an automated acknowledgement, and a profile view are not that step. An offer, or a clear invitation to a next round, is the sign that interviews are converting. A polite rejection after a final round is painful, and it is still information: you got further than a silent application.

You can be weak in more than one stage. Start with the earliest stage that is quiet. If you are not finding suitable roles, do not spend the week polishing interview stories. If interviews are happening and then stopping, do not spend the week blaming the file format.

The three logs below are illustrative. They are not a score, and they are not a rate you should copy. They show how the same feeling, "nothing is happening", sits on three different records.

*Illustrative logs. Read the stage, not a percentage*

- Maya Chen, software engineer

**Applications going out. No first conversation.** Fieldnote, backend engineer, CV B, applied, no next action. Harbour Transit, API role, CV B, applied, no reply. Paperlane, platform role, CV B, applied, acknowledgement only. The roles are similar. The result is the same.

- Sam Okonkwo, data analyst

**Conversations happening. No offer.** Linden Analytics, first round done, waiting. Westmere, case study, then a rejection with no detail. Orchard Systems, recruiter screen, then silence. The CV is already opening doors.

Maya does not yet have an interview problem. Sam does not yet have an application problem. Priya does not yet have a big enough set of real applications to judge the CV at all. If your notes look like one of these, stay with that stage for the rest of this guide.

**02**

## A diagnostic you can use

Work down the page. Stop at the first branch that describes your record. Do not average the three stages into a mood.

**Have you been finding roles you would actually accept?**

**If no**

You are stuck at finding suitable roles. A role counts only if you could do the core of the work, you meet any real barrier such as a licence or the right to work, and you would take it if they offered. Saved adverts you would decline do not count. Go to [finding suitable roles](#finding-suitable-roles).

**If yes**

**Have any applications you actually sent led to a first conversation?**

**If you have sent very few**

You do not have a pattern yet. Two applications, or a scatter of unrelated titles, cannot tell you that the CV has failed. Record what you sent and keep applying to similar suitable roles until the list can show a stage. Guessing a "normal" reply rate will not fill that gap. There is not a published rate that applies to your title, your level, and every employer.

**If a run of suitable applications has produced no conversation**

Look at shortlisting. "A run" means several applications for similar roles, sent properly, over a stretch of searching you choose, with the same kind of result. If the roles were random, the pattern is about targeting, which sends you back to finding. If they were similar and suitable, go to [getting shortlisted](#getting-shortlisted).

**If some applications have led to a conversation**

**Have any of those conversations led to a further round or an offer?**

**If you have had only one interview**

One interview is not a pattern. Note what you were asked, what you struggled to answer, and whether the role was at the right level. Then wait for a second data point before you rebuild your entire approach.

**If interviews keep ending without a next step**

The application is already doing part of its job. Go to [converting interviews](#converting-interviews). Also check that the roles match work you have done. A string of interviews for a scope you have never held is a finding problem wearing an interview's clothes.

**If some processes move forward**

You are not stuck in a general way. Look at what those processes had in common: the title, the CV version, the examples you used. Do more of that, and stop treating the rejections as proof that the whole search is broken.

Write the answer in a sentence you could say to a friend: "I am finding suitable roles and not getting conversations", or "I am getting conversations and not getting offers", or "I am not applying to roles I would accept." That sentence is the diagnosis. The worksheet at the end is where you turn it into one test.

**03**

## Finding suitable roles

Suitable does not mean perfect, and it does not mean every advert that uses your old job title. It means you can show evidence for the core of the work, you are not blocked by a genuine barrier, and the level is close to work you have done or can defend. The guide on [applying when you do not meet every requirement](../guide-apply-without-every-requirement/index.html) is the test for a single advert. Use it before you decide a role was suitable.

### What quiet finding looks like

- You refresh the same board and see the same unsuitable posts.

- Your saved list is long and your sent list is short.

- The titles jump around: junior one day, director the next, a different profession by Friday.

- You are filtering so hard, on a tool, a commute, or a single exact title, that almost nothing remains.

- You are only looking at public adverts, and the roles you want are filled through teams you have never spoken to.

[CareerOneStop, a careers site sponsored by the US Department of Labor, suggests a search plan built from short, specific tasks](https://www.careeronestop.org/JobSearch/Plan/create-a-job-search-plan.aspx): know the titles you are qualified for, research employers, and tailor each application. The useful part for this stage is the narrowing. A plan that says "apply to jobs" does not tell you whether Monday's advert was suitable.

### One change at this stage

Pick one of these. Leave the others alone until you have seen whether it changes the list of roles you would accept.

- **Name two titles, not ten.** Write the title you do now, or the closest honest step, and one adjacent title you could evidence. Search those. Drop the titles that need a scope you have not held.

- **Add one source you have not been using.** A company's own careers page, a professional community you already belong to, or a recruiter you have actually spoken with. One new source, used properly, beats five new tabs.

- **Loosen one filter that is hiding suitable work.** Keep the constraints that are real, such as a city you cannot leave or a clearance you must hold. Relax one preference, such as an exact framework or a fully remote rule, for a week, and see what appears.

- **Apply to the saved roles that pass the suitable test.** If the list is the problem, sending is the change. Do not rewrite the CV first.

A good week at this stage ends with a short list of roles you would accept, and with applications sent to some of them. It does not end with a new theory about the market.

**04**

## Getting shortlisted

You are here if suitable applications are going out and first conversations are not coming back. The CV, the form, and the match can all cause that. So can applying to roles that were never suitable. Check that last one before you redesign the document.

### Separate the causes

1. **Were the roles suitable?** If you missed a legal or licence barrier, or you could not evidence the core of the work, the silence is explained. Stop counting those applications as a test of the CV. Read 
- [apply, clarify, or skip](../guide-apply-without-every-requirement/index.html) and remove that kind of role from the next batch.

2. **Can a person see the evidence quickly?** Duty lines and adjectives do not help a reviewer who is comparing you with the advert. Replace "responsible for" with a true change in the work. The guide on 
- [achievements when the job has no obvious numbers](../guide-achievements-without-numbers/index.html) shows how to do that without inventing a percentage.

3. **Can the file be read?** A layout that hides your name, dates, or tools can produce a thin application even when the experience is real. Use the plain-text check in 
- [the guide to screening software](../ats-guide-v1/index.html). That check is not a score, and a clean file is not a promise of an interview.

4. **Did the form answer the eligibility questions honestly and completely?** A wrong or blank answer on the right to work, a required location, or a qualification can end the application before anyone weighs your projects.

5. **Are you comparing like with like?** If every application used a different CV, you cannot tell which version is quiet. Label the version you send. Change one version at a time.

Some application forms ask for a statement against published criteria, which is common at public bodies, universities, charities, and other employers that score answers rather than only reading a CV. If that is the form you are filling in, a strong CV will not rescue a statement that misses half the criteria. Use [how to write a supporting statement](../guide-supporting-statement/index.html) for that shape of application.

**InterviewAlpha tip.** Label the CV version you send, and keep that label on the application. [The CV vault shows which version is attached to which role](https://interviewalpha.app/?utm_source=guides&utm_medium=article&utm_campaign=guide-stuck-search&utm_content=inline-3). It will not invent a normal reply rate, because there is not one to invent.

### What to test

Choose one: a tighter definition of suitable, one rewritten CV aimed at the essential lines of a single title, or a file and form check on the next application. Send the next batch with that one change held still. If you change the title, the template, and the tone in the same week, you will learn nothing.

**05**

## Converting interviews

Interviews mean a person wanted to talk. That does not mean the interviews were at the right level, or that your examples matched the advert. It does mean the first place to look is the conversation, not a secret filter on the CV.

After each interview, write five lines while you remember them: the questions you could not answer cleanly, the example you wish you had used, anything they seemed to care about that was not on the advert, and whether you still wanted the role at the end. Also note the level. If they were hiring for a scope you have not held, say so in your notes. Repeating that pattern is a reason to change the roles you accept interviews for, not a reason to memorise a smoother introduction.

[The National Careers Service describes the STAR method](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice/the-star-method) as situation, task, action, and result, and suggests keeping examples short enough to say in a conversation. You do not need a new story for every possible question. You need a few true ones that match the requirements they keep repeating. If you only have an evening before the next interview, use [the one-evening preparation guide](../guide-interview-one-evening/index.html). It is a suggested schedule, not a rule.

Ask for feedback when a process ends, once, in a short note. Some employers will not reply. A reply that says "we went with someone who had done X" is useful if X is something you can show next time, or a sign that the role was the wrong level. It is not a score.

### Do not confuse these

- A recruiter screen that ends because of pay, location, or sponsorship is a condition, not a failed interview performance.

- A technical exercise you were not told about is a preparation gap. Note the format for next time. Do not treat one exercise as proof you cannot do the job.

- A final round that ends with another candidate is one outcome. Several final rounds at the right level, with the same weak answer, are a pattern worth practising.

**06**

## Worksheet: choose one change to test


Copy this into your notes, or print it and write on the lines. Fill it in for the stage you named above. Leave the other stages alone while you run the test.

**The quiet stage**

Finding suitable roles, getting shortlisted, converting interviews, or too few applications to tell.

**What my own record shows**

Roles I would accept, applications actually sent, first conversations, further rounds or offers. No borrowed rate.

**The one change I will test**

One sentence. A title list, a source, a filter, a CV version, a file check, or three interview examples.

**What I will leave alone**

Name the fixes you are tempted to pile on, and park them.

**What I will look at next time**

The stage, not a feeling. When I will read the record again.

**07**

## Frequently asked questions

### How many applications should I send before I change my CV?

There is no number that fits every title and every employer. If you have only sent a handful, or the roles were not similar, you do not yet know whether the CV is the quiet part. If a run of suitable, similar applications has produced no first conversation, then a single deliberate change to the CV is a fair test. Keep the previous version so you can compare them.

### Is a quiet search just the market?

Hiring slows, and some titles are crowded. That still does not tell you which stage is quiet in your case. A market explanation that stops you looking at unsuitable roles, unreadable files, or interviews at the wrong level is not a diagnosis. Use your record.

### Should I follow up on every silent application?

A silence after an application is common, as the National Careers Service notes. One short, specific follow-up is reasonable when the advert invited it or you have a named contact. A sequence of chasers will not repair a role that was never suitable, and it will not create an interview from a file that does not show the work.

### What if I seem stuck at two stages?

Test the earlier one first. Finding comes before shortlisting, and shortlisting comes before interview performance. Change one thing, keep a record, and only then look at the next stage.
